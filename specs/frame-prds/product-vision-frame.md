# Product Vision: Frame

**A governed work platform that thinks like Frappe and feels like Smartsheet**

Version 0.3, August 2026

---

## 1. The one-paragraph vision

Frame is a work management and low-code application platform where every table is backed by a real, governed data model. Users experience a Smartsheet: a grid they can type into, flip into Gantt, card, or calendar views, wire up with formulas and automations, expose through forms, and roll up into dashboards. Underneath, every grid is a Frappe-style Blueprint: a metadata-defined document type with typed fields, validation, role and attribute-based permissions, workflow states, an auto-generated API, and a full audit trail. Fields can bind to the organization's own master data and corporate facts in BigQuery, resolved under the reader's own credentials so the warehouse's access policies remain the only authority, which means a tracker references the real project register rather than a stale copy of it. The result is the adoption curve of a spreadsheet with the integrity of an enterprise system. It runs on our standard stack (React with Vite, Python, Firestore, Postgres, Cloud Run on GCP), speaks natively to Google Workspace, and integrates with Atlassian the same way Bob 2.0 already does.

## 2. Why build this

### The Smartsheet problem

Smartsheet wins adoption because it meets people where they are: a grid. But its unit of organization is the sheet, and a sheet has no semantics. A "project" in one team's sheet has 14 columns, in another team's sheet it has 40, and nothing in the platform knows they are the same kind of thing. Governance is bolted on afterwards through premium apps (Control Center to stamp out templates, DataMesh to sync copies, Dynamic View to hide columns) that exist precisely because the core model is semantically empty. Large Smartsheet estates become archaeology: thousands of sheets, forked templates, broken cross-sheet links, and no single answer to "show me all active projects."

### The Frappe problem

Frappe solved the semantics problem in 2005, and its own README is explicit about the lineage: "Started in 2005, Frappe Framework was inspired by the Semantic Web." Its DocType model means the system knows what a Project is, what a Task is, and how they relate. Define the metadata and you get forms, list views, REST APIs, reports, and workflow for free. The first application built on it, ERPNext, carries more than 700 object types, which is the evidence that a metadata model of this shape scales rather than collapsing under its own generality.

Permissions are the instructive exception, and it runs in our favour. What a DocType gives you declaratively is roles crossed with an integer field-sensitivity band and an is-the-caller-the-owner flag, plus per-user allow-lists of link-field values. Anything genuinely attribute-based — any operator other than equality, any cross-field condition, any date arithmetic — requires a hand-written hook returning raw SQL for list queries, plus a second, separate predicate for single-document access, kept in sync by hand. Frappe's real ABAC lives in code, in two languages, in two places. Our claim that ABAC rules live declaratively in Blueprint metadata and compile once to a single evaluation path is a substantive advance over the model we are borrowing from, which is also why that path needs a specified compiler rather than an assertion.

But Frappe is a framework for developers, and it shows in the two places that matter to us. The authoring surface is developer-shaped, not spreadsheet-shaped: ordinary staff will not model their work in DocType forms, and Frappe's own best editable grid, its Report View, has no clipboard paste, no fill handle and no undo stack, and pages rather than virtualising. The operating model is the second obstacle: bench, a site-per-tenant filesystem convention, resident background workers and a resident realtime process are a self-hosted shape, not a Cloud Run one.

Two things we should not say, because a reviewer who knows Frappe will discount everything else we claim. Frappe's README describes the stack as "Python and MariaDB on the server side", but the codebase carries first-class MariaDB, Postgres, SQLite and DuckDB backends, with per-backend DDL and sequence handling throughout — which matters to us, because Frappe's schema-sync, naming-counter and query-builder patterns are directly readable prior art for our Postgres side. And the Desk is not a server-rendered admin UI: the desk page ships an empty shell plus a metadata boot payload, and the entire interface is constructed in the browser from that metadata — architecturally the same "ship the metadata, render in the client" model we propose for React. We are not arguing that Frappe's data layer or its rendering model are wrong. We are arguing that its authoring experience and its operating model are wrong for us, and that its semantics are worth stealing. Frappe is a framework for developers. We need a product for everyone, with a framework underneath for us.

### The gap

Nothing on the market gives non-technical staff a spreadsheet-grade experience over a semantically governed data model. Airtable and Smartsheet are grids without governance. Frappe and traditional low-code platforms are governance without grids. AI makes this gap more expensive, not less: AI agents and analytics are only as good as the semantics of the data they read, and a pile of ad hoc sheets is the worst possible substrate for the agentic tooling we are building elsewhere (Playbook, Prism, Workflow Studio). Frame is the structured work substrate those platforms deserve.

### Why us, why now

We already run the ingredients. Prism gives us a semantic layer discipline. Playbook gives us AI orchestration, citation, and human review patterns. Bob 2.0 gives us proven Workspace and Atlassian integration patterns. Workflow Studio gives us BPMN-grade process tooling. The ABAC home page work gives us attribute-based access patterns. Frame is not a greenfield moonshot, it is the convergence layer: the place where structured operational work (registers, trackers, intake queues, project plans, portfolios) lives with the same rigor we demand from our transactional systems, without forcing everything into the ERP.

And we hold the one ingredient nobody can sell us. Every tracker in the organization is full of strings that should be keys: somebody types "Kenya CO" in one sheet and "KECO" in another, and the archaeology problem reappears one column at a time. The answer already exists in our own warehouse, and it is further along than most of us realise. `unops-datahub` publishes 569 dimension views and 393 fact views behind a curated interface layer, with a purpose-built metadata catalogue beside them: table and column descriptions, declared business keys, a DIMENSION-or-MEASURE classification per column, security policy tags, named data stewards for eight business domains, and a declared relationship graph of some 3,600 edges — 2,780 of them from facts to the dimensions that define their grain. Access to all of it is decided by BigQuery against each staff member's own Google identity.

So Frame does not copy that data, and does not re-express its access rules. A team lead defines a field as "the project this relates to," and it resolves against the real project master, governed by BigQuery rather than by a copy of it, with no second access-control list for anyone to maintain and drift. This is the one capability in the product that no external vendor can match, and the reason is structural rather than competitive: a SaaS platform outside our tenancy cannot run a query inside our warehouse as our staff member. What it can sell instead is a scheduled copy — which is precisely what Smartsheet's DataMesh is — and a copy leaves its entitlements behind at the tenancy boundary, to be re-expressed by hand, inside their product, forever.

## 3. Core concept: Blueprints wearing grids

The central design decision, stolen shamelessly from Frappe and dressed in Smartsheet's clothes:

**Every workspace object is an instance of a Blueprint.** A Blueprint is a metadata document (stored in Firestore) that defines fields with types and validation, relationships to other Blueprints, permission rules (role plus attribute based, consistent with our ABAC work), workflow states and transitions, computed fields and formulas, display defaults per view, and lifecycle policies (retention, archival, freeze).

**Blueprints compose into parent-child structures.** A Blueprint can declare one or more child Blueprints (a purchase memo with line items, a project with milestones and risks), and children can have children of their own where the domain genuinely needs it (a framework agreement with call-offs, each call-off with deliverables). This is Frappe's child DocType pattern generalized: a child row belongs to exactly one parent, participates in the parent's workflow by default, and travels with the parent through save, versioning, and document generation. Permissions compose rather than merely inherit: the parent's permission context is the ceiling on reach (nobody can access a child of a parent they cannot see, so a child row never leaks the existence of its parent), while a child Blueprint can define its own additional rules within that ceiling, including attribute-based rules keyed to the child row's own field values. The canonical example: a project record is visible to all project team members, but its child risk table is further gated so that rows with certain risk types are visible only to specific roles or named individuals. Effective access is therefore the intersection of parent access and child-level rules, evaluated by the same ABAC engine, and views, forms, APIs, and document generation all respect the composed result, so an embedded child grid renders only the rows and fields the viewer clears both gates for.

**Permissions are transparent to users.** Frame never pretends a trimmed view is the whole picture. When permission rules withhold rows or fields from a viewer, the interface says so: a risk table shows "12 risks, 3 high, 3 not visible to you," a trimmed column presents as restricted rather than absent, and rollups and dashboard metrics carry the same annotation. This resolves what would otherwise be a genuine design dilemma in aggregates (compute over the trimmed set and mislead about totals, or over the full set and silently leak that hidden rows exist) by refusing the premise that the gate must be invisible: aggregates are computed over the full set, honestly annotated. There is no need to hide from users the fact that permission gates exist; visible gates normalize deliberate, governed access, and they prevent the quiet failure mode where two people argue over different numbers without knowing why. The one deliberate exception: for the narrow class of Blueprints where the count itself discloses something sensitive (a single withheld row on a conduct register announces that a conduct case exists), stewards can enable existence masking per Blueprint or per rule. Transparency is the default; masking is a named, audited choice, never an accident of implementation. The UI follows the data model. A parent renders as a master-detail form (header fields on top, each child collection as an embedded grid below, expandable rows for grandchildren), while the same children are also reachable as flat grids in their own right when someone needs to work across parents ("all deliverables due this month, regardless of agreement"). This dual rendering, form-with-embedded-grids for transactional work and flat cross-parent grids for operational work, is something Frappe supports only as a report-builder special case: its Report View will flatten child rows across parents into an editable grid, but a child type there has no life of its own — no list route, no saved views, no board, no sidebar, and no permissions of its own, because Frappe deliberately clears a child type's permission rows and delegates every access question to the parent. Smartsheet cannot express the first at all: no column type creates line items, forms cannot create child records, and its Dynamic View details panel shows the fields of one row, not a child collection. Our contribution is that a child collection is a first-class Blueprint with its own views, its own rules within the parent's ceiling, and its own place in search and the generated API, while still travelling with its parent through save, versioning and document generation. This is a headline feature, not a footnote — and it is the one capability unmatched at any Smartsheet price tier.

**Every Blueprint automatically yields the full surface area.** Grid, Gantt, card, calendar, and timeline views. Master-detail forms for parent-child Blueprints. A typed REST and query API generated per Blueprint version, with OpenAPI published automatically — as against the incumbent, where every endpoint is generic (a rows endpoint returns untyped cell arrays keyed by column id, because there is no type to generate against), API access requires a Business plan or above, and it is unavailable on the large-scale sheets that exist for volume. Public and internal forms with conditional logic. Report and dashboard availability. Automation triggers. Audit logging. Import and export. Nobody builds a CRUD screen in Frame, ever. This is Frappe's "the best code is the one that is not written" applied with a straight face.

With one honesty caveat we build in rather than discover. Every Blueprint yields a grid, a master-detail form, a generated API, forms, reports, automation triggers, audit and search unconditionally. Gantt, calendar, board and timeline additionally require the Blueprint to designate which fields carry their dates, lanes and dependencies — a handful of dropdowns any user can set, not a developer task. A Blueprint with no dates honestly offers no calendar, and the view switcher names the missing field rather than rendering an empty view. Frappe gates the same views the same way, behind explicit field maps, and it is right to.

**Users never see the word Blueprint unless they want to.** A team lead creates "a new tracker," picks a template or describes it in plain language to the AI, and starts typing into a grid. Behind the scenes a Blueprint was created or extended. The semantics are the default, not the ceremony. Power users and IT can open the Blueprint editor and see exactly what the grid really is, version it, and promote it from team-local to organizational.

**Blueprints have a promotion ladder.** Personal, team, and organizational tiers. A personal tracker is free-form and fast. Promotion to team tier requires naming and typing the fields. Promotion to organizational tier goes through a light governance gate (IT or a domain steward reviews the model, deduplicates against existing Blueprints, and publishes it to the catalog). This ladder is the answer to shadow IT that neither bans experimentation nor lets it metastasize. It converts the Compass problem into a paved road.

**Attribution is built into the ladder.** Every Blueprint carries provenance metadata: who authored it, who contributed to revisions, and what it was forked from. When a Blueprint is promoted and published to the catalog, the credit travels with it, visibly: "Vendor Security Assessment Register, thanks to the work of Maria K., Infrastructure Practice." The catalog surfaces adoption stats (how many teams use it, how many rows it governs) alongside the credit line, so authorship of a widely adopted Blueprint becomes a legible professional contribution rather than invisible plumbing. People share what they get credit for. This costs almost nothing to build and is the difference between a catalog that grows and one that IT populates alone.

**Promotion is AI-assisted, not AI-decided.** The governance gate is where good intentions usually go to die under workload, so we tool it properly. When a Blueprint is submitted for promotion, an AI review assistant prepares the steward's working file: a similarity scan against the existing catalog (is this a duplicate of, or a near-variant of, something already published, with a field-by-field diff), field type and naming normalization suggestions aligned to our data standards, inferred validation rules from the actual data in the team-tier rows, flagged permission gaps (fields that look sensitive but are unrestricted), and a draft migration plan for teams already running look-alike trackers. The steward reviews, adjusts, and decides. The target is that a routine promotion takes a steward under thirty minutes of attention and clears in days, because a slow gate does not get bypassed politely, it gets bypassed.

## 4. Product pillars

### Pillar 1: The grid, done properly

The grid is the front door and it must be excellent: fast keyboard-first editing, fill-down, multi-select, frozen columns, row hierarchy with indent and outdent, cell comments, attachments per row, embedded child grids inside parent forms, and formulas with cross-Blueprint references (the governed replacement for Smartsheet's fragile cell links: references resolve through the data model, not through sheet coordinates, so they survive restructuring). View switching is instant and lossless: the same rows render as Gantt bars with dependencies, baselines, and critical path, as cards in a board grouped by any field, or as calendar entries. Views are saved, shared, and permission-trimmed, so two people opening the same tracker can legitimately see different columns and rows (Dynamic View as a native capability, not a premium bolt-on).

Alongside our own grid, Frame offers a **bound Google Sheets surface** as a secondary editing mode for spreadsheet power users. See section 5a for the design and its deliberate limits: our grid is canonical and governed, Sheets is an escape hatch with a contract.

### Pillar 2: Forms and intake

Every Blueprint can expose forms: internal forms for staff, branded external forms for partners and the public, with conditional logic, file upload, prefill, and child-table sections where the intake genuinely has line items (a request with multiple positions, an application with multiple attachments-plus-metadata). Forms are the intake edge of workflows, so a partner request submitted through a form lands as a row with a workflow state, an assignee, and an SLA clock, not as an email. This is where the Partner Personnel Portal and similar front-office patterns converge with internal operations.

### Pillar 3: Automation and workflow, two tiers, loosely coupled

Tier one is Smartsheet-grade no-code automation: when a row changes, when a date approaches, when a form arrives, then notify, assign, request approval, move state, update fields, generate a document. Built and owned by the team, executed entirely inside Frame, visible in an automation log.

Tier two is real workflow: Blueprint state machines with role-gated transitions inside Frame, and escalation to Workflow Studio for BPMN processes that span systems. The critical design constraint is that Frame and Workflow Studio must not become tightly coupled, and the way to guarantee that is to make the boundary an event contract rather than an embedding:

Frame publishes versioned domain events to Pub/Sub (`frame.row.created`, `frame.row.state_changed`, `frame.form.submitted` and so on) with a stable, documented schema and correlation IDs. It neither knows nor cares who subscribes. Workflow Studio, when a process is bound to a Blueprint, subscribes to those events like any other consumer, runs its BPMN logic, and acts back on Frame exclusively through Frame's public API with its own service identity, subject to the same permission evaluation as any client. No shared database, no direct service-to-service calls from Frame into Workflow Studio, no Workflow Studio internals leaking into Blueprint metadata. The only thing a Blueprint stores is a lightweight process binding (which external process is listening, for display and traceability), and the only thing Frame renders is process status read back through Workflow Studio's own API, shown as an informational panel on the row.

Graduation from tier one to tier two is then a data migration, not a rewrite: the tier-one automation's trigger conditions become the event subscription filter, its actions become the opening steps of the BPMN process, and Frame's behavior does not change at all. Frame remains fully functional if Workflow Studio is down (events queue, processes catch up), and Workflow Studio can orchestrate across Frame, the ERP, and anything else without Frame being aware. Both platforms stay independently deployable, independently versioned, and honest about their jobs.

### Pillar 4: Reporting, dashboards, and the Prism handshake

Frame ships row-level reports (filtered, grouped, cross-Blueprint joins through relationships, parent-child rollups) and dashboards with charts, metrics, and embedded views, because teams need answers where they work. But Frame deliberately does not grow an analytics platform. Organizational Blueprints replicate continuously into Postgres in analytics-friendly shape (children as proper related tables, not JSON blobs), and Prism's semantic layer reads from there. Team dashboards live in Frame, official analytics and cross-cutting insight live in Prism. One boundary, stated once, enforced in architecture.

### Pillar 5: Document generation

Rows become documents: letters, certificates, purchase memos, meeting packs, generated from templates with merge fields including child-table iteration (line items rendering as document tables), using the same TipTap-based template editor we are standing up for Playbook HTML outputs. Generated documents can route to e-signature and file themselves back onto the row and into the right Drive folder. One template system across platforms, not three.

### Pillar 6: The AI layer, two altitudes

AI in Frame runs at two altitudes, and the boundary between them is the same one our governance framework already draws.

**Native AI, in the product, for the everyday.** Frame embeds lightweight AI directly, calling models through our standard gateway without the ceremony of an orchestrated workflow: model from language ("I need to track vendor security assessments with a reviewer, a risk rating, and a 30-day follow-up" produces a draft Blueprint the user refines in the grid), formula and automation authoring from natural language, field value suggestions and cleanup (normalize these vendor names, extract dates from this pasted text), ask-this-view questions with answers cited to rows, and one-shot summaries (turn this filtered view into a status paragraph). These are interactive, user-initiated, low-stakes assists where the human is looking at the result before anything persists. Making users open a governed workflow to generate a SUMIF would be governance theater, and it would teach people to route around the platform.

**Playbook, for the repeatable and the consequential.** The moment AI becomes a repeatable pattern running as a process rather than an assist inside an interaction, it belongs to Playbook: agentic triage of incoming form submissions, scheduled portfolio summarization distributed to stakeholders, evaluation or assessment logic applied to rows, anything that acts autonomously, at scale, or in a domain our AI governance framework flags. Those run under Playbook's registration, human-review-by-default operating mode, and citation discipline, reading and writing Frame through its API and MCP surface. The rule of thumb we will state in the product documentation: if a person asks and immediately reviews, it is native; if a process runs and people rely on its output, it is Playbook.

Frame exposes Blueprints as MCP tools — and the protocol alone is not a differentiator, so we should not claim it as one: Smartsheet ships an MCP server too, generally available since March 2026 with connectors for Claude and, since June, for ChatGPT, Copilot and Gemini Enterprise. What is differentiated is what our tools return. Every Frame MCP tool is evaluated by the same permission library as the grid and the API, and every response carries transparency annotations, so an agent is told when it is reasoning over a trimmed set. An agent that silently reads a partial picture and confidently reports a total is the subtle failure mode of putting AI on governed data; the annotation is the fix, and nobody else has it. The second difference is semantic: their tools expose sheets, which are cells without meaning, while ours expose typed Blueprints, so an agent reading Frame knows what a risk rating *is*.

### Pillar 7: The app composer

The WorkApps analog, and the payoff of the Blueprint model: compose views, forms, dashboards, and pages into a role-scoped app with navigation and branding, no code. A "Fleet Management" app for one audience and a "Grants Intake" app for another, both reading the same governed Blueprints. This is our composable architecture vision (Grant+, Project+, Infrastructure+) given a floor to stand on: many of those "expert systems" are, honestly, Blueprints plus views plus workflow plus a domain rule library, and Frame makes that an afternoon of configuration instead of a project.

### Pillar 8: Corporate data, referenced rather than copied

A Blueprint field can be bound to organizational master data — a project, a country, a fund, a business unit, a supplier, a position — and to the corporate facts about them. The values come from the warehouse, not from a list somebody pasted in eighteen months ago, and the relationships between them come with the data: choosing a project narrows the cost-centre picker, and the attributes that hang off that project can populate the row without anyone re-keying them. This is the difference between a tracker that mentions a project and one that *knows* which project.

Three properties make it more than an integration. **The catalogue is discovered, not registered.** An administrator names a BigQuery project and, if they wish, excludes some datasets; a scheduled sweep reads the warehouse's own published metadata and keeps an always-current set of bindable dimensions, facts and relationships. There is no registration queue to become a bottleneck, which matters because a slow gate here would produce exactly the failure the promotion ladder exists to prevent: people typing the project name as free text.

**Access is decided by the warehouse, not re-expressed by us.** Where entitlement varies by person, the query runs under that person's own credentials, so BigQuery's IAM, row access policies and column policy tags are the enforcement point. Frame implements no rule about corporate data and holds no copy of anyone's entitlements. Where the warehouse has already ruled that every member of staff may see something, we serve it from our own store at grid speed, because executing per-user to reach a conclusion already known is theatre that costs latency and money. The line between those two cases is drawn by a mechanical probe of the warehouse's own policies, never by assertion, and the governing rule is simple enough to audit: **Frame caches no label that anyone may be denied.**

**And we never aggregate.** A corporate figure on a row reads a column that is already the number, at a grain the warehouse already publishes. Frame composes no aggregation of its own, on any path. If a number does not exist at a grain, that is a request to the data platform team for a mart — which is the correct home for it, because they own what "expenditure to date" means and we emphatically do not. This is what keeps section 7's fence a mechanism rather than a promise.

## 5. Architecture on our stack

**Frontend.** React with Vite. The grid is the hard part and gets a dedicated engineering investment: a virtualized grid component owned by us (evaluating TanStack Table plus custom virtualization versus AG Grid licensing as an explicit early decision), a shared view layer for Gantt, board, and calendar, master-detail form rendering generated from parent-child Blueprint metadata, and real-time presence and co-editing delivered through a Frame-owned realtime tier (Cloud Run supports session affinity). Clients subscribe to server-defined rooms — row, Blueprint, workspace, user — and subscription to a room is itself a permission decision, evaluated by the single permission library before the subscription is accepted, with payloads on a room trimmed by that same library. **Clients hold no direct Firestore listeners on row data.** This is not a performance preference, it is the only shape consistent with our own rule that there is exactly one permission implementation: a client listener would force row-condition and field-set access into Firestore security rules, which is a second implementation in a second language, and one that cannot express parent-child composition or typed restricted stubs at all. Frappe's realtime tier is the shape to copy — a separate socket process fed by pub/sub, whose default permission answer is a call back into the application tier precisely so the socket tier needs no database access.

**Backend.** Python services on Cloud Run. A metadata service (Blueprint CRUD, versioning, provenance and attribution, promotion, catalog), a data service (row CRUD including parent-child transactions, validation against Blueprint metadata, permission trimming, formula evaluation), an automation engine (trigger evaluation, action execution, Cloud Tasks for scheduling, Pub/Sub for events, and the published event contract that Workflow Studio and others subscribe to), a document generation service, an integration service (Workspace, Atlassian, webhooks, the Sheets binding service), and an AI gateway serving the native tier directly and delegating governed work to Playbook. All APIs generated from Blueprint metadata, OpenAPI published automatically, consistent with the Frappe pattern.

**Data.** Firestore holds Blueprints, rows for document-shaped and hierarchical data (parent-child structures map naturally to subcollections, with the parent as the transactional boundary), comments, presence, and activity feeds. It is the system of engagement: fast, real-time, flexible schema, which is exactly what user-defined models need. Postgres holds what genuinely needs relational integrity and query power: organizational-tier Blueprint replicas for reporting and Prism (children normalized into related tables), cross-Blueprint reference integrity for promoted models, formula dependency graphs at scale, and full-text search indexes. The honest rule: Firestore for engagement, Postgres for integrity and analytics, replication from the former to the latter as Blueprints climb the promotion ladder. We do not pretend one database does both jobs well.

That split deserves a defence rather than an assertion, because "one relational database would halve the operational surface" is a reasonable objection. The single-store alternative fails in one of two ways. A table per user-defined Blueprint means thousands of schema migrations authored by end users, in a product whose promotion ladder exists specifically to encourage schema experimentation. An entity-attribute-value model surrenders exactly the query power that was the reason to choose Postgres in the first place. It is also worth noting that even Frappe no longer holds the single-store, single-process line: four database backends, and a separate resident realtime process.

One invariant governs the split and must not be eroded under deadline pressure: **Firestore is the system of record for every tier; Postgres holds derived state only; nothing in Postgres is authoritative; everything in Postgres is rebuildable by event replay; and no user-facing write path writes to Postgres directly.** That rule is what distinguishes this from a dual-write architecture, and it is the first thing a future engineer in a hurry will be tempted to break.

**Corporate data.** A discovery service sweeps the warehouse's published metadata on a schedule and maintains the catalogue of bindable dimensions, facts and relationships. Reads that could vary by person execute under that person's own credentials, through a BigQuery scope obtained by the same user-consented connector pattern Bob 2.0 already uses for Workspace; reads of data the warehouse has declared unrestricted are served from a narrow projection in our own Postgres. Frame's project submits and is billed for warehouse queries, which is what makes bytes-billed ceilings, required partition filters, per-workspace cost attribution through query labels and a circuit breaker possible at all — the user's identity still decides what comes back. The analytics replica carries corporate keys only, never copies of dimension data: the warehouse already holds it, and joining there is the natural act.

**Security and governance.** Google Identity via IAP and OAuth, ABAC permission evaluation in the data service using the attribute patterns from our home page work, row and field level trimming server-side (never in the client), immutable activity log per Blueprint and per row, retention and archival policies as Blueprint metadata, and export controls for external collaborators. Chrome Enterprise Premium watermarking and DLP apply as they do to the rest of our estate. Data residency is settled by construction: it is our GCP, our region, our keys (CMEK where warranted). Note that this is a true description of Frame and not, on its own, an argument against buying — see section 10.

## 5a. The Google Sheets question

Should the grid itself be an embedded Google Sheet? The idea is tempting: Sheets is a world-class grid we get for free, our users already live in it, and we are a Workspace organization. It deserves a straight answer rather than a reflexive no, so here is the assessment.

**Why Sheets cannot be the canonical grid.** Four structural reasons, none of them fixable with cleverness. First, permission trimming: Frame's promise is that two users opening the same tracker see different rows and columns based on ABAC evaluation. A Google Sheet has one body of cells; anyone who can open it sees all of it. Protected ranges and hidden columns are advisory, not security. The moment restricted data touches a Sheet, field-level governance is fiction. Second, schema enforcement: Sheets validation is decorative. Users can paste over it, insert columns, break the mapping, and the sheet will happily let them. We would spend our lives writing reconciliation code for drift we cannot prevent, and as you note, the sheet remains editable outside the app entirely. Third, the event model: Frame's automations, workflow events, and audit trail need reliable, attributed, row-level change events. The Sheets API offers polling and coarse onChange notifications without guaranteed delivery or per-cell attribution at the fidelity an audit trail demands. Fourth, the UI ceiling: saved permission-trimmed views, embedded child grids, Gantt and board morphing, presence, row comments tied to workflow, none of it can be built inside an iframe we do not control.

**Why Sheets is still worth a first-class integration.** Everything above argues against Sheets as the system of record. None of it argues against Sheets as a satellite surface. So the recommendation is exactly the shape you suggest at the end of your framing: we build our own grid and make it excellent, it is the canonical, governed surface and the only one supporting dynamic views, and alongside it we offer **bound Sheets** as a deliberate, contract-governed working mode:

A user with sufficient permission on a view can "Open in Sheets." Frame generates a Sheet from that view (only the rows and columns that user is entitled to see, which neatly sidesteps the trimming problem: the trim happens at generation), tagged with hidden binding metadata and registered in a binding service. The binding has a mode. **Snapshot** is export, full stop, no sync back. **Live-read** refreshes the Sheet from Frame on a schedule or on demand, formulas and pivots on top are the user's business, edits do not return. **Round-trip**, the interesting mode, watches the bound range, and treats edits coming back not as writes but as *proposed changes*: each inbound edit is validated against the Blueprint (types, validation rules, permissions of the person who made the edit, resolved via Drive revision attribution), applied if clean, and queued as a reviewable exception if not, with conflicts against edits made in Frame in the meantime surfaced explicitly. In other words, a round-trip Sheet is architecturally an import channel with a live cursor, and it reuses the import pipeline's validation machinery rather than pretending to be a second canonical store.

Round-trip mode is gated: available on team-tier Blueprints and on organizational Blueprints only where the steward enables it, never on fields marked restricted, and always visible in the row activity log ("changed via bound Sheet by X"). If a user mangles the sheet structure, the binding degrades gracefully to snapshot and tells them why.

**What this buys us.** Spreadsheet power users keep the tool where their fluency lives for bulk edits, offline-ish work, and personal analysis, and their route back into Frame is governed rather than being an email attachment titled `tracker_FINAL_v7.xlsx`. Adoption resistance drops because Frame is not asking anyone to abandon Sheets, only to let Frame hold the truth. And we get it comparatively cheaply, because the hard parts (validation, permissions, conflict handling) are the import pipeline we need anyway.

**What it must never become.** The escape hatch must not quietly become the front door. If telemetry shows a team living entirely in bound Sheets, that is a product signal that our grid is failing them, to be fixed in the grid, not accommodated with deeper Sheets coupling. The performance budget and quality bar for our own grid stand unchanged; Sheets binding is a bridge for users, not a crutch for us.

## 6. Integrations

**Google Workspace, deep not decorative.** Drive: each workspace maps to a Drive folder, row attachments are Drive files with Drive permissions mirrored, generated documents file themselves. Sheets: the bound-Sheets modes described in 5a, plus plain export of any view. Docs: embed live Frame views in Docs via smart chips where the API allows. Gmail: create rows from emails, send from automations with reply threading back onto the row's conversation. Calendar: date fields sync to team calendars, resource bookings both ways. Chat: notifications, approvals actionable from Chat, a Frame Chat app for quick queries. Groups: permission principals resolve Google Groups so access management stays where admins already do it. All through the OAuth and connector patterns proven in Bob 2.0.

**Atlassian.** Jira: bidirectional link between Frame rows and Jira issues, so a portfolio Blueprint in Frame can roll up delivery status from engineering's Jira without anyone re-keying, and an intake row can spawn a Jira issue with state sync. Confluence: embed live Frame views in Confluence pages, generate Confluence pages from rows (meeting packs, decision records), and link Blueprints to their Confluence documentation.

**Everything else.** Webhooks in and out on every Blueprint, the generated REST API, the published Pub/Sub event contract, an import and export pipeline (CSV, Sheets, Smartsheet migration tooling as a deliberate feature), and MCP server exposure for the agent ecosystem.

## 7. What we are explicitly not building

Numbered so they can be cited rather than paraphrased, because section 10 names scope gravity as a top risk and this section is what gets quoted back. Each carries the requirement that implements it.

**N1. No email client, no chat platform, no document editor.** Workspace exists. Frame sends notifications and generates documents from templates; it does not become a place you read mail or author prose. *(PRD 12, PRD 07.)*

**N2. No analytics platform.** Prism exists, and the boundary is architectural rather than aspirational: team dashboards live in Frame, official analytics live in Prism, and team-tier data is deliberately not replicated so that promotion is the path to Prism-grade analysis. *(RP-10.)*

**N3. No BPMN designer.** Workflow Studio exists, behind a published event contract. Frame publishes domain events and renders process status read back through Workflow Studio's own API; no external system blocks a Frame state transition. *(AU-8, AU-11, AU-13.)*

**N4. No AI orchestration stack.** Playbook exists. Frame keeps only interactive, human-reviewed assists native; anything autonomous, scheduled, or bulk-affecting routes to Playbook, and the boundary is enforced in product rather than documented. *(AI-7.)*

**N5. No scripting in team automations, at any tier.** The action vocabulary is closed on purpose, because that is what keeps graduation to Workflow Studio mechanical. Both reference implementations agree from opposite directions: Frappe ships its Server Script disabled behind a site-config flag outside the application, a dedicated role, a restricted interpreter, read-only SQL and stripped transaction control; Smartsheet's answer to the same pressure is a premium tier that runs JavaScript. Where the pressure is real, it goes to the code-first action library or to a platform-owned validator, never to a user-authored runtime. *(AU-3, AU-3a.)*

**N6. No cell-anchored formulas.** A per-cell expression site is a column whose meaning varies by row: no Blueprint describes it, no API can type it, no report can aggregate it honestly, no document merge can resolve it, and it breaks silently on row insert. Migrants get field formulas, reference-path formulas, rollups, and bound Sheets for genuinely personal analysis. *(GR-22, BP-9, BP-9a, BP-10.)*

**N7. No unauthenticated write surface beyond published forms.** External participation in v1 is one-way — published forms and magic-link status pages, never editing an existing row — which is narrower than Smartsheet, where a free guest shared as editor can edit specific items. What is exposed is inventoried rather than assumed. *(FM-5, FM-8, PM-13.)*

**N8. No second permission implementation, and no evaluation input outside the compiled rule set.** Not in the client, not in store security rules, not in the realtime channel, not in the search index, not in the replica's consumers, not in MCP. There are no per-row grants; access comes from compiled rules only, which is what makes compile-once evaluation sound and access review complete by construction. *(PM-4, PM-4a, PM-2a.)*

**N9. No moving or copying rows between Blueprints, and no free-form outbound HTTP.** Both exist in Smartsheet because a sheet has no semantics and data must be physically relocated to change its meaning. Here the answer is a view filter, a state change or a reference; physically moving governed rows would break provenance, audit continuity and reference integrity. Outbound calls go to allowlisted, signed webhook destinations. *(IN-12.)*

**N10. No aggregation over corporate data, and no charting over it.** A corporate figure reads a column that is already the number at a grain the warehouse publishes. Frame composes no `GROUP BY`, no join, no window function and no aggregation function on any path. A number that does not exist at a grain is a request to the data platform team for a mart, not a feature request for Frame. Charting over warehouse data is Prism's, per N2. *(PRD 14.)*

**N11. No resource and capacity management, and no creative proofing.** Both are distinct product categories — Smartsheet bought a company for the first — and neither is made cheaper by having a semantic core.

**N12. No public SaaS ambitions in v1.** This is an internal platform first, though the architecture (per the front-office code reuse pattern) keeps a path open for offering it to sister agencies later, which given UN80 and shared services conversations is worth keeping honest. Note that our unit of tenancy is the *workspace inside one tenant*, not the tenant — an assumption that hardens fastest in exactly the subsystems Phase 1 builds, so it is recorded here deliberately.

## 8. Success looks like

Twelve months in: the top 20 spreadsheet-based operational registers (risk registers, vendor trackers, asset inventories, intake queues) run on organizational Blueprints. New trackers default to Frame because it is genuinely faster than making a sheet. The Blueprint catalog is the answer to "do we already track this," shadow trackers surface through the promotion ladder instead of through audit findings, and the catalog's credit lines have made Blueprint authorship something people mention in their performance conversations. Prism reads governed operational data instead of scraping exports. Playbook agents act on structured work with permissions intact.

The corporate-data measure is the sharpest one available to us, because it is countable rather than felt: **the proportion of columns naming a corporate entity that hold a key rather than typed text.** Today that number is effectively zero across the estate's spreadsheets, and every point of it is a reconciliation somebody no longer has to do by hand. A second, equally countable: how many teams have stopped maintaining their own copy of a reference list.

And the honest metric: staff choose it without being told to, because the grid is good.

## 9. Phasing

**Phase 1 (foundation, roughly two quarters).** Blueprint engine including single-level parent-child, the grid with saved views and conditional formatting, master-detail forms, forms intake including repeatable child sections, tier-one automations, comments and attachments, Workspace identity and Drive integration, activity log, provenance and attribution metadata, import from Sheets, CSV and Excel, Sheets snapshot export. Corporate data arrives here in its cheap half: source registration, the discovery sweep, and lookup fields against dimensions the warehouse has declared unrestricted — no consent screen and no warehouse query on any read path. Pilot with three real registers owned by friendly teams, at least one with line items to exercise parent-child.

**Phase 2 (work management).** Gantt with dependencies and baselines, board and calendar views, cross-Blueprint formulas and relationships, multi-level and multiple children, dashboards, document generation with child-table iteration, Gmail and Calendar and Chat integration, the promotion ladder with the AI-assisted steward review, the catalog with credit lines, bound Sheets live-read mode, the published event contract. Corporate data gains relationship-constrained pickers and carried attributes, so choosing a project narrows the cost-centre list and populates what hangs off it.

**Phase 3 (platform).** App composer, Jira and Confluence integration, Postgres replication and the Prism handshake, MCP exposure, Playbook-tier AI processes, Workflow Studio process bindings over the event contract, bound Sheets round-trip mode, Smartsheet and legacy tracker migration tooling. Corporate data completes with the per-viewer half — dimensions whose entitlement varies, resolved live under the viewer's own credentials — and with corporate figures, gated behind the cost apparatus that half genuinely requires.

## 10. Risks, named plainly

**The grid is the product.** If the grid is 80% as good as Smartsheet's, adoption dies and everything above it is architecture nobody visits. This is the single largest engineering risk and deserves our best frontend people, an early spike, and ruthless performance budgets (10,000 rows, sub-100ms interactions). The bound-Sheets mode is a pressure valve for users, not an excuse for us: if people flee to Sheets, the grid has failed and the grid gets fixed.

**Governance friction kills the ladder.** If promotion to organizational tier takes weeks, people stay at team tier forever and we have rebuilt Smartsheet's sprawl with better fonts. The AI-assisted steward review exists to make the gate fast; the steward function still needs real resourcing, and the thirty-minutes-of-attention target should be measured, not assumed.

**Round-trip Sheets drift.** The round-trip binding is the riskiest integration surface: revision attribution in Drive is coarser than we would like, and reconciliation edge cases will be ugly. It ships last (Phase 3), behind steward opt-in, degrading to snapshot on structural damage, and if it proves more trouble than adoption value, we keep snapshot and live-read and drop round-trip without regret.

**Scope gravity.** Every stakeholder will ask Frame to become the ERP, the analytics platform, or the workflow engine. Section 7 exists to be quoted back at them.

**Formula engine complexity.** Cross-Blueprint formulas with real-time recalculation over Firestore is genuinely hard. We should scope v1 formulas conservatively (row-level, parent-child rollups, and single-relationship aggregation) and treat the dependency graph engine as its own workstream.

**Build versus buy scrutiny.** The honest counter-argument is "just buy Smartsheet Enterprise." Two of our arguments have to be dropped, because they are procurable from the incumbent and a procurement reviewer will know it: data residency and key custody. Smartsheet sells regional instances (US, EU, Australia, Gov) and customer-managed encryption keys held in the customer's own KMS. "Our GCP, our region, our keys" is a true description of Frame; it is not an argument against buying.

What survives, and is checkable. First, the semantic core: Smartsheet's column type system has no reference type, no rollup type and no formula type, so cross-object meaning can only be expressed as text plus a formula plus a convention. Second, real parent-child documents: no column type creates line items, forms cannot create child records, and Dynamic View's details panel shows the fields of one row, not a child collection. Third, ABAC consistent with our estate: core sharing is item-scoped with five levels and no row or field granularity, and the add-on that retrofits row and field access starts at $125 per month on Business and above — and does not render in their native mobile app, which is the tell of something bolted on. Fourth, Playbook-governed AI under our own framework. Fifth, the composable substrate for Grant+ style products. And sixth, the one that is structural rather than competitive: **corporate data referenced in the user's own context.** No external vendor can execute a query inside our warehouse as our staff member. What they sell instead is a scheduled copy whose entitlements stay behind at the tenancy boundary and must then be re-expressed by hand, inside their product, forever.

Three published limits make the semantic argument concrete rather than adjectival. A sheet caps at 20,000 rows, 400 columns or 500,000 cells. A sheet may hold at most 100 distinct cross-sheet references. And the Enterprise "large-scale sheet" mode that raises the row cap to 50,000 does so by disabling automated workflows, reports, forms, connectors, DataMesh, Pivot, proofs, search, the mobile apps and the public API — and it does not support cross-sheet references either, so the volume escape hatch and the cross-sheet escape hatch are mutually exclusive. Past 20,000 rows Smartsheet stops being a platform and becomes a table; our budget is 10,000 rows at 60fps and 50,000 via windowed fetch with every platform capability intact.

We are not rebuilding Smartsheet. We are building what three of its premium apps exist to compensate for: Control Center (provisioning by folder copy, with Global Updates as a distributed schema-migration tool for those copies), DataMesh (the foreign key implemented as a scheduled lookup-value copy), and Dynamic View (row and field permissions). We make no such claim about DataTable, Data Shuttle, Pivot, Bridge, Resource Management or Brandfolder, which address capacity, ETL, analytics, orchestration and asset management — real problems that a semantic core does not make disappear.

**Corporate data inherits the warehouse's governance, for better and worse.** Delegating access decisions to BigQuery is the right design and it has a dependency we cannot engineer around, so we should say it out loud now rather than hear it from an auditor: Frame will faithfully propagate whatever governance BigQuery actually has. If a dataset is over-shared, Frame will over-share it accurately. This is an argument for concentrating that investment in one place rather than maintaining a second copy of it in every application — and it will be the first time we have a mechanical, continuous report on how good that governance is, which is worth having whatever it says. The related operational risk is that an automatically-discovered catalogue moves without a human deciding: a dataset retired or re-pointed upstream can silently strand a dimension that thousands of rows reference. Detection is instant and free; remediation is a scheduled migration with a real downstream cascade, and the two must never be conflated into a single "fix it" button.

**Warehouse query cost is a new and genuinely unbounded spend line.** BigQuery bills by bytes scanned, our project pays, and the difference between a picker aimed at a curated forty-megabyte relation and one aimed at a two-gigabyte view is the difference between a rounding error and a six-figure monthly line. Nothing in the estate has ever set a bytes-billed ceiling, a dry run or a query label, so this discipline is greenfield. It is also why the phasing is drawn along the cost seam: the large, common, cheap half ships first with no warehouse query on any read path, and the expensive per-viewer half ships behind the controls it needs.

## Appendix: A note on workflow graduation

Pillar 3 claims that graduation from a tier-one automation to a Workflow Studio process is a data migration rather than a rewrite. Here is what that migration actually looks like.

The claim rests on one design fact: a tier-one automation is not code, it is a structured record. When a team builds "when Status changes to Submitted and Amount > 50,000, then assign to the category lead, request approval, and notify the requester," Frame stores that as data: a trigger definition (event type: `row.state_changed`, conditions: `status = Submitted`, `amount > 50000`) and an ordered list of action definitions (assign, approval request, notification), each with its parameters. The automation engine just interprets this record when matching events flow past.

Now suppose that approval process outgrows tier one. Procurement wants a second approval layer above a threshold, an SLA escalation, and a step that touches the ERP, which is Workflow Studio territory. Graduation means Frame takes the existing automation record and translates it mechanically into the starting artifacts of a Workflow Studio process.

The trigger definition becomes the **event subscription filter**. Recall from the coupling design that Workflow Studio listens to Frame's Pub/Sub events. The automation's trigger was already expressed in terms of those same events (`row.state_changed` with conditions), so it exports directly as the subscription filter that starts the BPMN process. Same event, same conditions, different consumer.

The action list becomes the **opening lane of the BPMN process**. Assign, request approval, notify are all primitives Workflow Studio can express as service tasks calling back through Frame's public API. So the generated process definition opens with those three steps, faithfully reproducing what the automation did yesterday. The process owner then extends it in Workflow Studio with the things tier one could never do: the second approval gate, the timer-based escalation, the ERP call.

The final step is a swap: Frame deactivates the tier-one automation and records the process binding on the Blueprint, so the row now shows "governed by process X" instead of "automation Y." From the end user's perspective on day one after graduation, nothing changed. The row still gets assigned and the approval still arrives. What changed is where the logic lives and what it can grow into.

That is the sense in which it is a migration rather than a rewrite. Nobody re-derives the business logic from memory or reverse-engineers what the automation did; the trigger and actions were data, and data can be exported into the richer format. Contrast the usual pattern, where "graduating" a Zapier-style automation to a real workflow engine means a business analyst interviewing the team about what the automation does, then rebuilding it by hand and hoping the two behave identically during the cutover.

Two honest caveats. The translation is a starting point, not a finished process: it hands the process owner a BPMN skeleton that matches current behavior, and the value of Workflow Studio only materializes when they extend it. And the mapping only stays mechanical if tier-one actions remain a closed, well-defined vocabulary that Workflow Studio has matching service tasks for, which is an argument for keeping the tier-one action set deliberately small and resisting requests to make team automations Turing-complete. The moment tier one grows scripting, graduation becomes a rewrite again, and the whole ladder loses its rung.
